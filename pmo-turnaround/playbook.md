# The 90-Day Playbook

**Status:** 🚧 In progress

An inherited PMO is not a greenfield design. The sponsor may want more control, not less. Finance and audit may depend on data the PMO produces. Executives use the dashboard. Many of the people were hired to collect status. Some of them are in other countries. A big plan from a new leader reads as a takeover. This playbook is written for those conditions.

## By day 90, three deliverables

1. **The stop list, done.** Reports and meetings the PMO no longer runs, actually stopped, not announced.
2. **One major program visibly unblocked,** with the decision it was waiting on.
3. **A signed charter** with one hard lever, a money ledger, the governance tiers and kill criteria for the turnaround itself.

Everything else waits until months 4 to 9. A low-trust function cannot absorb eleven changes in a quarter.

## Weeks 1 and 2: contract and protect

**Contract with the sponsor.** In the sponsor's own words: what problem are they paying to fix, and what would they see in six months? If the answer is more control, lead with the parts of the model that serve control: dependency records, a decision log, a gate on the riskiest work. Write the agreed outcome into the charter. If two sponsors disagree, settle it with them before talking to the teams.

**Check the data dependencies.** Ask Finance which data it takes from the PMO today (budget tracking, time data used to capitalize software work). Ask internal audit and security which PMO artifacts count as control evidence. Nothing gets stopped until you know.

**Meet the PMO's own people.** What is changing, what is not decided yet, and when it will be. Make no promises about roles.

## Weeks 3 to 6: diagnose

- **Separate conversations** with the PMO staff, engineering leaders, product leaders, security and finance. Ask each the same questions: What do you think we are doing? What are we actually doing? What are we supposed to be doing? Where does it break: people, time, money or communication? What would you stop tomorrow? What would you miss?
- **Find the trust failure type** for each group. Do they doubt the PMO's ability (late, wrong, slow), its benevolence (accurate but costly to them), or its integrity (spin, policing)? The repair is different for each.
- **Inventory** every report and recurring meeting the PMO owns, with the hours it costs.
- **Sample the tracker data** on the biggest programs, for example 50 items each, and work out how much of it is wrong before any of it feeds a dashboard.
- **Map who benefits from the current PMO.** Some managers use its status as their own upward report. Some executives like steering committees. They will resist losing them.

## Weeks 7 to 13: act

- **Execute the stop list,** after the data check.
- **Unblock one major program** and make the decision it needed visible.
- **Sign the charter:** mission, what the PMO does and does not do, the tiers, the lever, the money ledger, the kill criteria, and the date it gets reviewed.

## Staff in other countries

- Default to written, asynchronous decision requests with a 24-hour response clock.
- Put one site lead in each country with real authority.
- Rotate meeting times so the same people are not always up at night.
- Run the trust survey per site.
- In some cultures, reporting red to a distant headquarters carries career risk. A site lead who backs people up makes red safe. A slogan does not.

## Months 4 to 12

- **Months 4 to 6:** two pilots under the new model, each with stated criteria for stopping. Governance tiers live. Correction-of-error reviews on misses. The new dashboard runs beside the old one.
- **Month 6:** kill-criteria review with the sponsor. Continue, change course, or stop.
- **Months 6 to 9:** retire the old dashboard. Finish the people plan. First quarterly note to leadership on the three systemic causes slowing the portfolio, with the evidence and the decision each one needs.
- **Months 9 to 12:** review the money ledger with Finance. Forecast in ranges where the data allows it. Retire the first mechanisms that no longer earn their place. Measure how often teams ask for a TPM without being told to.
- **Year two:** standing changes, or it does not. Keep the charter current and the sponsor close. A change of sponsor resets the clock.

## Kill criteria for the turnaround

At month six, change course if any of these is true:

- The sponsor will not sign the lever, or has stopped coming to the portfolio review.
- The pilots' forecasts are no closer to actuals than the baseline was.
- Teams report no drop in reporting load, measured in hours.
- The money ledger shows no capacity freed and no spend avoided.
- Trust has fallen on any of the three traits in two or more partner groups.

## The PMO's own people

- Do not promise that nothing changes. If status collection is going away, say so.
- Assess skills with HR against the new role definitions in the first 60 days.
- Publish the roles, for example TPM for the biggest programs, portfolio and planning, delivery data and tooling, and vendor management.
- Give each person a real path: a role, a development plan into one, or an honest conversation with notice. Say what will be decided by when.
- Follow the legal and consultation steps each country requires before announcing anything.
- If the span of control is too large, design the management layer after the assessment and announce it once.

## What to say

**To the executive and the CFO:** "Here is what this office costs today, and here is the ledger I will report against every quarter: capacity we free by stopping or merging work, spend we avoid, and the cost of delay on our five biggest programs. There is no good evidence that program offices in general improve delivery, so I am not asking you to believe in one. I am asking for a charter with one lever and two pilots with criteria for stopping at month six."

**To engineering:** "I can't show you a study proving a PMO makes delivery faster, and I won't pretend to. So here is the test: for every artifact we add, we remove one, and we measure what it costs your teams. If a meeting we run doesn't end in a decision, it goes. If you're stuck on something outside your team for a day, it comes to us."

**To the PMO staff:** "Some of what this team does today is going to stop, because systems can do it or nobody uses it. I won't tell you nothing changes. I will assess skills with HR against the new roles, publish those roles, and give each of you a real path. You'll know what is being decided and by when."
