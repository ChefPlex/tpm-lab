# The 90-Day Playbook

**Status:** 🚧 In progress

An inherited PMO is not a greenfield design. The sponsor may want more control, not less. Finance and audit may depend on data the PMO produces. Executives use the dashboard. Many roles in the PMO were designed around collecting status. Some of them are in other countries. A big plan from a new leader can feel like a takeover, even when it is not meant as one. This playbook is written for those conditions.

## By day 90, three deliverables

1. **The stop list, done.** Reports and meetings the PMO no longer runs, stopped in practice and not only announced.
2. **One major program visibly unblocked,** with the decision it was waiting on.
3. **A signed charter** with one hard lever, a money ledger, the governance tiers and the criteria for changing course on this improvement plan itself.

Everything else waits until months 4 to 9. While trust is still being built, eleven changes in a quarter is more than any team can absorb.

## Weeks 1 and 2: contract and protect

**Contract with the sponsor.** In the sponsor's own words: what problem are they paying to fix, and what would they see in six months? If the answer is more control, lead with the parts of the model that serve control: dependency records, a decision log, a gate on the riskiest work. Write the agreed outcome into the charter. If two sponsors disagree, settle it with them before talking to the teams.

**Check the data dependencies.** Ask Finance which data it takes from the PMO today (budget tracking, time data used to capitalize software work). Ask internal audit and security which PMO artifacts count as control evidence. Nothing gets stopped until you know.

**Meet the people in the PMO.** Tell them what is changing, what is not decided yet, and when it will be. Be honest that roles are not settled, and do not promise outcomes you cannot keep.

## Weeks 3 to 6: diagnose

- **Separate conversations** with the PMO staff, engineering leaders, product leaders, security and finance. Ask each the same questions: What do you think we are doing? What do you see us doing day to day? What are we supposed to be doing? Where does it break: people, time, money or communication? What would you stop tomorrow? What would you miss?
- **Find which kind of trust needs work** for each group. Is the concern about ability (late, inaccurate or slow information), benevolence (accurate, but costly to them), or integrity (reports that do not match what they see, or oversight that feels like checking up on them)? The repair is different for each.
- **Inventory** every report and recurring meeting the PMO owns, with the hours it costs.
- **Sample the tracker data** on the biggest programs, for example 50 items each, and measure its error rate before any of it feeds a dashboard.
- **Map who relies on what the PMO does today.** Some managers use its status as their upward report. Some executives value their steering committees. Plan what replaces each one before anything stops.

## Weeks 7 to 13: act

- **Execute the stop list,** after the data check.
- **Unblock one major program** and make the decision it needed visible.
- **Sign the charter:** mission, what the PMO does and does not do, the tiers, the lever, the money ledger, when to change course, and the date it gets reviewed.

## Staff in other countries

- Default to written, asynchronous decision requests with a 24-hour response clock.
- Put one site lead in each country with real authority.
- Rotate meeting times so the same people are not always up at night.
- Run the trust survey per site.
- Across distance, and in some workplace cultures, reporting red to headquarters can feel like a career risk. A site lead who backs people up makes red safe. A message from headquarters alone does not.

## Months 4 to 12

- **Months 4 to 6:** two pilots under the new model, each with stated criteria for stopping. Governance tiers live. Correction-of-error reviews on misses. The new dashboard runs beside the old one.
- **Month 6:** review with the sponsor of when to change course. Continue, change course, or stop.
- **Months 6 to 9:** retire the old dashboard. Finish the people plan. First quarterly note to leadership on the three systemic causes slowing the portfolio, with the evidence and the decision each one needs.
- **Months 9 to 12:** review the money ledger with Finance. Forecast in ranges where the data allows it. Retire the first mechanisms whose cost is now higher than their value. Measure how often teams ask for a TPM on their own initiative.
- **Year two:** make the changes permanent. Keep the charter current and the sponsor close. A change of sponsor resets the clock.

## When to change course

At month six, change course if any of these is true:

- The lever is still unsigned, or the sponsor no longer attends the portfolio review.
- The pilots' forecasts are no closer to actuals than the baseline was.
- Teams report no drop in reporting load, measured in hours.
- The money ledger shows no capacity freed and no spend avoided.
- Trust has fallen on any of the three traits in two or more partner groups.

## People in the PMO

- Be clear about what will change. If status collection is moving to systems, say so early.
- Assess skills with HR against the new role definitions in the first 60 days.
- Publish the roles, for example TPM for the biggest programs, portfolio and planning, delivery data and tooling, and vendor management.
- Give each person a real path: a role, a development plan into one, or, where no role fits, an honest conversation with fair notice and support. Say what will be decided by when.
- Follow the legal and consultation steps each country requires before announcing anything.
- If the span of control is too large, design the management layer after the assessment and announce it once.

## What to say

**To the executive and the CFO:** "Here is what this office costs today, and here is the ledger I will report against every quarter: capacity we free by stopping or merging work, spend we avoid, and the cost of delay on our five biggest programs. Published research does not yet show that program offices in general improve delivery, so I am not asking you to take that on faith. I am asking for a charter with one lever and two pilots with criteria for stopping at month six."

**To engineering:** "I cannot show you a study proving a PMO makes delivery faster, and I will not pretend to. So here is the test: for every artifact we add, we remove one, and we measure what it costs your teams. If a meeting we run does not end in a decision, we stop it. If you are stuck on something outside your team for a day, bring it to us."

**To the PMO staff:** "Some of what this team does today will stop. Systems can now do part of it, and some reports no longer change any decision. That is about how the work was set up, not about how well you did it. I will not tell you nothing changes. I will assess skills with HR against the new roles, publish those roles, and give each of you a real path. You will know what is being decided and by when."
